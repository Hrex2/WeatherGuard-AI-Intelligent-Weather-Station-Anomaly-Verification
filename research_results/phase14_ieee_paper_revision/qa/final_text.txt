Provenance-Aware Evidence Review of Weather-Station Anomalies Using Model Stability, Temporal Consistency, and Spatial ContextHarnoor Singh Thiara[Institution to be confirmed][City, Country]Abstract—An unusual weather-station reading does not prove a sensor fault. We audit WeatherGuard AI, which records model stability, temporal screening, spatial comparisons, and physical checks separately. On 8,415 held-out timestamps and 160 synthetic cases, E1 duplicates baseline votes, E2 flags 1,661 timestamps, E4 flags none, and E3 has no eligible independent observations. The baseline-driven policy marks 123 suspicious and 8,292 insufficient. Forty-two of 100,980 predictions disagree; the cause is unresolved. Without adjudicated fault labels, performance is unknown. This work contributes an auditable evidence-review method.Index Terms—Anomaly detection, meteorological quality control, uncertainty, data provenance, station validation, spatial corroboration.IntroductionWeather stations provide observations for weather monitoring, climate analysis, and local decision support. Records can contain abrupt spikes, gradual drift, stuck values, missing blocks, and physically implausible combinations. Automated screening is valuable, but a statistical anomaly is only a departure from a learned or specified reference. It is not, by itself, evidence of sensor failure. We adopt the rule ANOMALY ≠ FAULT: a candidate requires contextual evidence and, where evidence is insufficient, the system should abstain from a fault conclusion.Meteorological quality control combines range, step, persistence, internal-consistency, and spatial checks. Network studies emphasize metadata, instrument exposure, maintenance, calibration, and quality flags alongside algorithms [1]–[4]. Unsupervised methods add scalable screening, but their outputs depend on representation, parameter settings, training period, and contamination by faults [5]–[9]. We assess an evidence workflow that exposes model stability, temporal history, spatial context, and physical plausibility as distinct dimensions rather than claiming a new universally accurate detector.This repository-grounded study examines the saved baseline, temporal and physical screens, controlled synthetic responses, and a multi-phase spatial-provenance investigation. Phase 12 tests evidence coverage, overlap, dependency, and ablation while preserving the baseline policy. Results distinguish verified artifact counts from descriptive signals and unresolved validation gaps. Estévez et al. review validation procedures for automatic weather-station measurements [2]. Network standardization emphasizes metadata, sensor siting, maintenance, and harmonized quality assurance [3]. Faybishenko et al. discuss challenges in quality assurance and processing of heterogeneous meteorological time series [4], while Stawowy et al. model information quality from weather-station sensors [10]. These studies motivate treating source lineage and variable semantics as part of the evidence.Isolation Forest isolates observations through random partitioning [5]; Local Outlier Factor compares local density with neighboring records [6]; and One-Class SVM estimates a boundary around training data [7]. These methods do not estimate fault probability without appropriate labels and calibration. General anomaly and time-series surveys document the dependence of results on task definitions, data, and evaluation protocols [8], [9]. WeatherGuard uses configuration agreement as E1 stability, not calibrated confidence.Spatial agreement is not a universal truth condition: real gradients, elevation, siting, sensor height, sampling intervals, and shared upstream products affect inter-station comparisons. E3 eligibility therefore depends on source identity, time, variable comparability, and independence. This is a focused narrative review, not a systematic search or meta-analysis.Dataset and Data ProvenanceThe primary modeling artifact, `verified_phase01/model_df.csv`, is a processed historical series covering 2021-01-05 through 2026-03-09. An evidence ledger stores source, time, variable, quality/comparability, support or conflict status, and missingness. UNAVAILABLE is missing or ineligible evidence; CONFLICTING is disagreement from a comparable source; SUPPORTING is evidence consistent with the stated screen. These states are not diagnoses. Fig. 2 depicts this separation./Fig. 2. E1–E4 dimensions and interpretation limits. Components remain separate; E3 is outside classifier voting and is not validated for event adjudication here.E1: Model StabilityE1 summarizes agreement among 12 configurations: four each for Isolation Forest (IF), Local Outlier Factor (LOF), and One-Class SVM (OCSVM). A timestamp is a candidate when the number of flags meets a selected threshold. Agreement measures sensitivity to these configurations, not probability or independent voting; models share data, features, and timestamps.E2: Temporal ConsistencyE2 compares candidates with prior same-station history under configurable window, mode, minimum-observation, and deviation settings. SUPPORTING, NO_DETECTED_EVIDENCE, CONFLICTING, and UNAVAILABLE describe screen outcomes. No detected evidence is not evidence of station health; insufficient history is not a negative result.E3: Spatial CorroborationE3 compares eligible observations across stations by variable and time. Eligibility requires lineage, compatible units/report semantics, acceptable timestamp alignment, and adequate independence. Exact and tolerance-window matches are separate. Regional agreement may contextualize a signal; disagreement needs spatial representativeness and measurement uncertainty. E3 records context only and cannot alter classifier flags.E4: Physical PlausibilityE4 applies broad bounds, missingness, and selected cross-variable checks. Rules require sensor-, unit-, climate-, and instrument-specific validation. A violation does not distinguish sensor failure from transmission error, conversion error, or rare real conditions.Event interpretation and abstentionThe event-level unit is a bounded candidate episode, not one flag. In Phase 12, SUSPICIOUS follows the saved 8/12 baseline candidate flag; all other timestamps are INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE. No NORMAL, ANOMALOUS, or fault label is assigned. E2 and E4 do not vote, and descriptive E3 is decision-ineligible. Without independent evidence or adjudicated reference, the result remains uncertain or unadjudicated.Methodology and Experimental DesignThe analysis was artifact-first: saved CSVs, manifests, reports, source code, and documentation were inspected; figures were generated from saved artifacts. No model was retrained, no weather observations were downloaded for this drafting task, and historical outputs were not overwritten. Results distinguish processed-data description, saved screen output, synthetic response, and provenance/coverage investigation. None yields adjudicated fault performance.E1 and E2 analysesThe saved grid contains 12 configurations × 8,415 test timestamps = 100,980 cells. E1 thresholds are 4/12 through 12/12; none was selected using independent labels. The E2 sensitivity file spans 123 candidates, five windows (1, 3, 6, 12, 24 h), three cutoffs (2.5, 3.5, 4.5), and two modes, totaling 3,690 combinations. A separate E2 synthetic paired review contains 2,880 setting-level rows across 18 settings, with 160 underlying pair identifiers repeated across settings; those rows are not independent experimental units.Synthetic campaignFive selected contexts and eight injections—dewpoint inconsistency, gradual drift, intermittent noise, missing block, persistent offset, point drop, point spike, stuck sensor—form 160 context × scenario × injected-time units. We report corrected 8/12 E1 response and between-context ranges. Fixed injections do not estimate field recall or generalize to unobserved stations or fault patterns.E3 and E4 evaluationExact UTC matches are separated from tolerance matches, and valid-value pairs from common timestamps. Phase 6 comparisons failed the lineage eligibility gate. Phase 9 selected eight events; none was suitable for event validation. Phases 10/11 obtained no provider-ID-confirmed independently adjudicated fault or clean event. E4 uses saved broad physical-screen responses. Parity mismatches are reported without relabeling historical outputs or inferring a cause.Experimental ResultsDataset and station coverageThe primary table has 44,879 rows, 44,849 feature-ready; train/test partitions have 36,434/8,415 rows. E1 summarizes the same saved model labels as the baseline: Phase 12 found identical vote counts and 8/12 flags for all 8,415 timestamps. It therefore adds no separately identified input in this implementation. E2 supplies a distinct temporal-screen profile (1,661 support flags), but its validity and incremental predictive value are unknown without independent adjudicated events. Paired association statistics are descriptive and do not show that the evidence sources are independent.E3 cannot yet provide validated independent spatial corroboration. Earlier phases document station-record comparisons and limited report-system distinctions, but exact sensor lineage, independent source identity, variable comparability, event-level alignment, and adjudication are incomplete. E3 remains decision-ineligible and outside classifier voting. E4 produced no positive flags and found no missing measured-variable values in the Phase 12 window; this outcome neither proves that observations were fault-free nor establishes that the screen is ineffective. Ablation category stability follows from a decision rule that remains baseline-driven; evidence profiles change and zero category changes do not imply component equivalence.Limitations and Threats to ValidityThe primary series is a processed snapshot. Its manifest verifies file integrity and split dimensions, not original provider lineage or instrument history. The legacy Meteostat notebook omitted explicit model and flags settings; its 6,024-row outputs cannot be reconciled to later source-stratified exports or treated as direct observations. Independent, provider-identified and independently adjudicated sensor-fault labels are unavailable. Consequently, accuracy, precision, recall, F1, false-alarm rate, and fault-detection performance are not estimable.Further limitations include E1 dependence on baseline labels, shared processed primary-station inputs for E2/E4, zero E4 positive flags in the evaluated window, unresolved E3 source independence and timestamp alignment, one principal station, one temporal holdout, and synthetic injections on five selected contexts. The 42/100,980 Isolation Forest mismatches remain unexplained. The 8/12 threshold and screening rules are not independently calibrated. Future studies require raw reports with stable identifiers, verified station and sensor histories, prespecified event adjudication, cross-station and forward-time evaluation, and a reproducibility audit of estimator and preprocessing artifacts before performance claims.Conclusion and Future WorkWeatherGuard AI establishes a provenance-aware workflow for conservative review of suspicious weather-station observations. It keeps configuration stability, temporal screening, physical checks, and spatial context distinguishable; documents when evidence is missing or ineligible; and exposes structural dependence in the saved model screen. The present artifacts do not establish validated event-level spatial corroboration, sensor-fault detection, or superior predictive accuracy. ANOMALY ≠ FAULT.Next work should obtain provenance-verified independent station observations, preserve provider report identifiers and instrument metadata, adjudicate candidate events under a prespecified protocol, resolve the Isolation Forest parity mismatch, and evaluate calibrated screens across stations and time. These are proposed experiments, not completed results.AcknowledgmentThe author acknowledges the maintainers of project software and the providers of station metadata and archive interfaces.
